## Entries
### S1.1 What the Objectives section actually asks for, clause by clause
- **question:** What does the challenge require, what does it merely permit, and what does it leave open, taken clause by clause from the page text?
- **reader:** The build team on day one of a four-day event; may have skimmed the challenge card and needs the difference between a suggestion and an expectation.
- **entry:** A decision-support app idea, an agent-heavy build with components tested in advance, and a challenge page written almost entirely in permissive verbs.
- **point:** The Objectives use "may" for every dataset, crop and method, and harden into a definite requirement in only four places: combine Earth observations with local soil, crop, and farmer information; compare at least three rotation strategies including the current one; for each strategy explain why it was suggested, the evidence, the benefits, the information gaps and the risks needing local review; and present the result as support that does not replace farmer judgment.
- **chain:** Count the modal verbs in the quoted text; note the four places the modal disappears; note the Potential Considerations open with "you may (but are not required to)"; conclude that meeting the four definite clauses is the floor, and that because capacity is not the binding constraint the permissive list becomes the ceiling to reach for rather than the work to skip.
- **principle:** In a challenge written as permission rather than specification, grade the definite clauses first, because a team that satisfies the permissive list and skips a definite clause has done the unrequired work.
- **instance:** A team could use five of the eight named datasets and still miss the requirement by offering two rotations, or three rotations with no risk column.
- **limit:** The four definite clauses are still checked by humans with local knowledge, so satisfying them mechanically with empty evidence does not pass; the page also warns that resources may be added before the event, so the definite set can grow.
- **budget:** 460 words.
- **boundary:** This subsection owns the clause reading and the floor test; S1.2 owns judging evidence; S1.3 owns where authority sits.

### S1.2 How the challenge is judged, and what that rewards
- **question:** What does the 2026 site publish about judging, what does the archived rubric add, and what follows for a four-day build?
- **reader:** Same team, deciding how to spend scarce hours between data plumbing and polish.
- **entry:** A suspicion that a beautiful map wins, and no visible rubric on the 2026 site.
- **point:** The 2026 site publishes the process and withholds the weights, so no score can be predicted; the rubric words have been stable for a decade and include validity and impact, which a demo can only carry with traceable data handling and a farm-count story.
- **chain:** The awards page names nominees, finalists and honorable mentions but no criteria; the archived pages list impact, creativity, validity or product, sustainability and presentation, with impact defined by how much and how many; the event gives two days of build; conclude that validity and impact are the two levers a short run can move, and that polish moves presentation only.
- **principle:** Where a rubric exists but its weights do not, build to the rubric's named dimensions rather than to aesthetics, and say plainly that weights are unpublished.
- **instance:** One traceable rotation comparison over a named real field, with seasons, sources and uncertainty exposed, plus a count of how many farms and hectares the method could serve, carries validity and impact; a dashboard with no comparison carries presentation alone.
- **limit:** Local events add their own prizes and can diverge from global judging, so a local win is not evidence of global fit; the rubric is corroborated from archived pages and not from a 2026 statement.
- **budget:** 420 words.
- **boundary:** This subsection owns the judging evidence; S1.1 owns the ask; S1.3 owns the deferral boundary.
- **source:** `/research/challenge/challenge-page.md` §§Judging, as published, The event

### S1.3 Where the tool must defer to the farmer and the agronomist
- **question:** Which parts of a rotation choice stay with the farmer or agronomist, and how does the app say so without reading as a disclaimer?
- **reader:** Product owner and any agronomist reviewer; knows remote sensing is coarse but may still over-claim.
- **entry:** A map and agent interface built on proxies, modelled states and a soil layer with gaps.
- **point:** The challenge names non-replacement as an outcome, and the datasets justify it: no product here establishes rotation-caused yield, profit, soil carbon or input cost, and the tightest local constraints sit with a lender, a landlord or a co-op. Deference therefore has to be built into the answer, not appended to it.
- **chain:** The Objectives close on non-replacement; T1 shows every product observing a proxy at best; T3 and T4 put tenure, credit and co-op terms outside the satellite entirely; therefore each strategy card must name the field-level facts the tool cannot see and the person who holds them.
- **principle:** A decision-support tool earns trust by marking the boundary of its own evidence at the point of each claim, because a boundary disclosed once at the end is read as boilerplate.
- **instance:** A suggested rotation card that says which of its inputs were satellite-derived, which came from a soil survey, and which three facts only the farmer can confirm, named as facts rather than as a warning.
- **limit:** Deference cannot become paralysis: a tool that defers on everything gives nothing, so each strategy still has to commit to a ranked reason and a confidence level.
- **budget:** 420 words.
- **boundary:** This subsection owns authority and handoff; S1.1 owns the ask; S2 owns the per-sensor limits that justify deference.
