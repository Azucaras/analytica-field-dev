### S5.1 The safrinha window sets the whole rotation
- **question:** How does the first soybean crop determine whether a second crop can be planted and harvested inside the local weather, credit and insurance window?
- **reader:** Brazilian producers and app designers who know soy-maize succession but may treat crop rotation as an annual crop list rather than a timed sequence.
- **entry:** A Cerrado farmer must choose soybean sowing date and maturity group while reserving enough harvest and planting capacity for a second crop before the dry season.
- **point:** In the Cerrado, the soy harvest date is a first-order control on the second crop; a recommendation that ignores calendar, ZARC and local conditions can propose a crop outside its feasible window.
- **chain:** Soy sow date and cultivar maturity determine harvest date; harvest date and machine availability determine second-crop sowing; sowing affects flowering and grain-fill rainfall exposure; ZARC compliance links the applicable municipality/soil/cultivar window to agricultural custeio credit. In a 2018 Santa Carmem-MT experiment, maize sown 19 January yielded 2.33 Mg/ha more than maize sown 27 February; the paper gives about 20 February as a location-specific practical upper bound, not a Brazil-wide date. Satellite time series can help estimate crop timing and cover, but not confirm cultivar, harvest plan, loan condition, insured status or whether the grower will plant a second crop.
- **principle:** Represent the crop sequence as a dated, location-specific calendar with explicit uncertainty and alternative crops; do not convert a local trial date into a national deadline.
- **instance:** Use the Santa Carmem-MT trial and contrast the Dois Vizinhos-PR 2016/17–2017/18 maturity-group study: MG 6.0 sown mid-September allowed mid-February maize planting there, whereas soybean sown 15 October made maize uneconomic regardless of maturity group. State the trial site, years and crop; do not transfer the PR maturity group to Cerrado.
- **limit:** Weather, harvest delay, soil, crop variety and geography change the window. A late maize crop is not automatically the right alternative: sorghum, millet, sesame, sunflower, brachiaria and crotalaria have different markets, water use and pest effects. Satellite-derived planting dates are estimates and cannot settle ZARC, finance or insurance eligibility.

### S5.2 Credit, ZARC and PROAGRO as rotation constraints
- **question:** How can credit eligibility and agricultural risk protection remove a technically possible crop from a Brazilian farmer's feasible choices?
- **reader:** Brazilian growers, lenders and app designers needing the difference between a public credit envelope, a loan actually disbursed, a sowing-risk rule and insurance or loan protection.
- **entry:** A grower considers a later or higher-risk safrinha crop but must confirm the credit line, local ZARC window, insurance/protection eligibility and available working capital.
- **point:** Formal credit and insurance constraints can bind crop timing and risk, but published programme totals or a satellite-derived planting date cannot confirm a particular farmer's eligibility or loan access.
- **chain:** Plano Safra 2026/27 became effective 2026-07-01 with a R$525.1bn commercial plan and controlled rates including 9% Pronamp and 12.5% commercial cost, but the envelope does not guarantee an individual drawdown. T5 reports July–October 2026 rural-credit disbursements of R$107.6bn, down 25.7% year on year, including nearly R$20bn of renegotiations rather than new working capital. ZARC observance became a broader agricultural custeio-credit condition effective 2025-07-01. PROAGRO's R$200,000 enrolment ceiling was verified in T4 through CMN Resolution 5.224 effective 2025-07-01; T5 notes secondary reporting only, so prefer the primary T4 source and distinguish the unresolved T5 lead. PSR premium subsidy availability can also vary with budget blocks. Satellite data can characterize weather or crop development, not underwrite credit or establish a policyholder's coverage.
- **principle:** Display date, programme, eligible line, borrower conditions and verified source status separately; require lender/insurer confirmation before treating an option as financed or insured.
- **instance:** Compare planned Plano Safra rates with the 2026-07 to 2026-10 disbursement report and use PROAGRO's verified cap as a loan-protection example. Explain that the June 2026 PSR budget block was reported at R$461.6m of R$991.1m (46.5%), but the 2025 program still covered 3.2m ha; this is an availability/timing risk, not proof that every farmer lacked insurance.
- **limit:** National or crop-year amounts do not show farm eligibility, bank appraisal, credit timing, ZARC exceptions, coverage map or a particular indemnity. T4's primary CMN resolution should govern the cap; T5's “secondary report” label is a pack inconsistency to flag, not a reason to repeat the weaker status as fact. A satellite signal cannot resolve private lender decisions.
- **budget:** 640 words.
- **boundary:** This subsection owns formal credit, ZARC and protection eligibility. S5.1 owns crop-calendar mechanics; S5.3 owns freight, barter and farmgate netback; S5.4 owns winter crop weather and market choices.
- **source:** T4, “Rural credit and Plano Safra” lines 87–91; “PROAGRO loan protection” lines 93–97; “Rural insurance subsidy (PSR)” lines 99–103; “ZARC compliance” lines 105–109; “Second crop timing” lines 221–227. T5, “Brazil rural-credit volume and access” lines 154–159; “Plano Safra 2026/27” lines 182–189; “PROAGRO coverage limit and rural insurance rules” lines 191–196.

### S5.3 Freight, barter and the Mato Grosso netback
- **question:** What remains of an attractive crop price after farm-specific freight, delivery, barter and input-credit terms are accounted for?
- **reader:** Cerrado farmers, traders and app designers who need to distinguish export-market prices from the value and terms received by a specific farm.
- **entry:** A Mato Grosso operator compares soybean, second-crop maize and alternatives against local routes, grain delivery commitments, input credit and cash availability.
- **point:** A national or port price is not the farm's net return; route, timing, financing and the buyer's settlement can change which rotation pays.
- **chain:** Conab's September 2026 freight survey put Sorriso–Santos at R$500/t over 1,961 km, Sorriso–Miritituba at R$280/t over 1,076 km and Sorriso–Paranaguá at R$490/t over 2,128 km. Origin and route matter even within Mato Grosso: the pack lists Querência–Santos R$450/t and Primavera do Leste–Santos R$410/t. Fertilizer import volumes declined 15.7% for Jan–Sep 2026 versus the same 2025 period, a market signal rather than a specific farm's input rate. Barter advances inputs against future grain and can bind both source and buyer; 2024 industry figures reported R$12.6bn barter credit and R$80.8bn supplier lending, but CropLife has a commercial interest. Satellite data can map crop production and route geography, not invoice freight, basis, loan terms, price locks or a co-op's settlement.
- **principle:** Calculate a farmgate netback from dated farm-specific route, delivery and credit terms; label route-survey and industry figures by source and do not imply all farms receive them.
- **instance:** Use the September 2026 Sorriso route comparison to show that nearer Miritituba freight differs from Santos/Paranaguá, then explain how barter can finance a crop while reducing supplier and buyer choice. T4 also documents that bank-financed farmers in a two-municipality study had more input and sales options than barter farmers; keep the locality and age of that evidence.
- **limit:** Conab's route survey is not a farm's invoice or current contract; no unified Mato Grosso freight exists. The barter-credit totals are 2024 figures reported in 2025 by an industry source, not an individual loan quote or causal estimate. Farm-level realized basis and terms were not found.
- **budget:** 640 words.
- **boundary:** This subsection owns farmgate netback, freight and tied input/grain finance. S5.2 owns programme credit and insurance eligibility; S5.1 owns the second-crop calendar; S5.5 owns property geolocation and environmental compliance.
- **source:** T4, “Freight to port and basis” lines 26, 141–145; “Fertilizer cost and investment cutback” lines 26, 135–139; “Barter and input-supplier credit” lines 34, 189–193; “Cerrado” lines 203–208; “What I could not find” line 239. T5, “Brazil rural-credit volume and access” lines 32–33.

### S5.4 No-till tradition, frost and the southern system
- **question:** What constrains winter crop and rotation choices in Paraná and Rio Grande do Sul, where no-till is established but winter economics, timing and weather still vary?
- **reader:** Southern Brazilian growers and app designers who might mistakenly apply Cerrado soy–safrinha logic to PR/RS or equate no-till with a complete conservation rotation.
- **entry:** A southern producer chooses wheat, canola, barley, maize, cover or fallow after a summer crop while balancing sowing, frost, harvest rain, quality, insurance and local buyer access.
- **point:** In the South, a mature no-till baseline does not decide the winter crop; crop-specific price-cost, sowing/harvest weather, insurance and co-op or mill channels shape the next sequence.
- **chain:** Embrapa's October 2026 report states 45.8 Mha under no-till and 6.4 Mha (14%) meeting all three pillars; no-till alone does not establish permanent cover or crop diversification. In 2026 PR wheat area was 722,000 ha, down from 820,000 ha in 2025, while safrinha maize reached 2.84 Mha; RS wheat was 763,121 ha in 2026 versus 1,166,163 ha in 2025, and canola expanded to 379,887 ha. Deral's January 2026 price/cost reference put wheat at R$62.19 per 60-kg sack, near estimated cost of 56 sacks/ha and 2025 mean yield of 57 sacks/ha. Frost risk at flowering and rain/quality loss at harvest differ by date and field; subsidy or co-op delivery can enable a choice, but do not guarantee its return. Satellite data can observe vegetation/cover signals, not field-level sowing intent, crop quality, local contract or insurance enrollment.
- **principle:** Separate the conservation-practice label from the actual rotation and cover pillars; evaluate southern winter crops with local calendar, price/quality, weather risk and buyer/insurance confirmation.
- **instance:** Contrast the 2026 PR wheat/safrinha maize area and RS wheat/canola area changes with the Deral near-break-even wheat reference. Use Coamo's 2025 results reported in 2026 (R$1.30 per 60-kg sack wheat rebate) only as a named co-op channel example; identify Coamo as an interested source and not a farmer quotation or universal benefit.
- **limit:** State totals do not reveal individual farm switches; crop areas and yields have different sources and reporting dates. An Embrapa three-pillar measure is not a yield ranking. Weather injury depends on crop stage and local conditions; no national farm-level insurance or independent winter-crop margin panel was found. The pack does not provide a direct named farmer quote for this subsection, so do not invent one.
- **budget:** 640 words.
- **boundary:** This subsection owns southern winter crops, no-till system completeness, frost/rain and local co-op channel. S5.1 owns Cerrado safrinha timing; S5.2 owns formal programme rules; S5.3 owns freight and barter; S5.5 owns legal reserve, CAR and EUDR.
- **source:** T4, “No-till and the rotation deficit” lines 29, 159–163; “Winter crop opportunity cost” lines 30, 165–169; “Wheat price-cost squeeze” lines 31, 171–175; “Frost, rain and insurance in southern wheat” lines 32, 177–181; “Co-operative relationship and patronage” lines 33, 183–187; “Region notes” lines 210–215.

### S5.5 EUDR, the CAR and the legal reserve
- **question:** How do property-level land rules, CAR status and export-chain traceability shape which fields can be financed or sold into particular markets?
- **reader:** Brazilian landholders, tenants, lenders, traders and app designers who need to distinguish property registration, legal reserve obligations and an export operator's due-diligence duty.
- **entry:** A producer considers a crop or buyer while the property has a biome-specific legal reserve, CAR record, credit screening and, if relevant, an EU-bound commodity chain.
- **point:** Satellite mapping can support parcel and land-cover evidence, but it cannot establish legal compliance, validate CAR, prove a commodity lot's origin or decide whether a crop is available under the landholder's legal and contract position.
- **chain:** Forest Code legal-reserve minimums depend on biome and property location: 80% for forest properties, 35% for Cerrado in the Legal Amazon, 20% for campos gerais in the Legal Amazon and 20% elsewhere, subject to statutory provisions. CAR registration is not validation, title or proof of field-level segregation; the pack's end-2025 reporting counted 8,154,567 registrations but only 480,700 completed analyses, a secondary status source. CMN Resolution 5.193/2024 makes CAR registration and environmental screening relevant to rural credit. EUDR covers in-scope EU-bound soy, requires geolocation and due diligence, and by the November 2026 snapshot had scheduled application dates of 2026-12-30 for large/medium operators and 2027-06-30 for micro/small operators. The operator placing goods on the EU market carries the direct legal duty; a satellite map is evidence input, not the due-diligence statement.
- **principle:** Treat property status, field boundary, buyer chain and legal review as separate data objects; disclose what a map can document and require landowner, lender or buyer confirmation for legal status and acceptance.
- **instance:** Contrast a 1,000-ha Cerrado property illustration (35% legal-reserve minimum before other provisions) with a CAR registration and a hypothetical EU-bound lot. The example is an illustration of statutory difference, not a recommendation or finding that a particular farm is compliant. EUDR dates in the pack are scheduled, not already enforced as of 2026-11-10.
- **limit:** Reserve rules have exceptions and property-history provisions; registration count does not prove validated properties. EUDR applies to covered EU-bound chains, not every domestic or China-bound tonne. CAR polygons, embargo checks, parcel-to-silo matching and buyers' contracts require authoritative records and human review. The private Soy Moratorium status mentioned in the pack is explicitly not settled by a URL-verified, unread item and must not be presented as law.
- **budget:** 640 words.
- **boundary:** This subsection owns land/environmental status and traceability. S5.3 owns farmgate market economics; S5.2 owns credit conditions; S5.1 owns calendar and agronomy. Do not claim satellite observation alone settles legal eligibility.
- **source:** T4, “Legal reserve and land-use opportunity cost” lines 22, 111–115; “CAR registration and credit gate” lines 22, 117–121; “EUDR traceability” lines 24, 123–127; “Cerrado” lines 203–208; “What I could not find” lines 231, 237–240.
