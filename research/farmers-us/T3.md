Older extension and adoption studies are marked with their year. `(read)` means the cited source text was opened; `(URL verified, not read)` means URL/metadata or a search result was checked, but not the full source; `(discovery only)` means a search result only. ## Per driver

### Cash rent and land cost

**Mechanism.** A fixed cash rent is a hard per-acre hurdle: it must be covered before the tenant earns a return. The 2025 USDA average was $161/ac for cropland, but state means ranged from $39.50 in Montana to $346 in California; irrigated land averaged $244/ac and non-irrigated $147/ac. Illinois' 2026 mean was $261/ac, while high-productivity central Illinois cash rent in the 2026 budget was $327/ac. Those figures justify local budgeting, not a general conclusion that rent always selects corn. (read; USDA-NASS 2025 Land Values/Cash Rents, Dec 2025; farmdoc, 2026-09; https://www.nass.usda.gov/Publications/Highlights/2025/2025LandValuesCashRents_FINAL.pdf ; https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2026/09/illinois-cash-rents-in-2026-and-outlook-for-2027.html ; https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2026/01/projected-incomes-on-owned-vs-rented-farmland-for-2026.html)

**Numbers and who pays.** In the farmdoc 2026 central-Illinois high-productivity example, 50/50 corn-soy generated $312/ac operator-and-land return before land cost; cash rent at $327/ac left a projected −$15/ac farmer return, while a 50/50 crop-share cost of $294/ac left +$18/ac. The tenant bears the fixed-rent deficit; a crop-share landlord bears part of crop-year downside. These are budget projections, not observed farm returns. (read; farmdoc, 2026-01; https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2026/01/projected-incomes-on-owned-vs-rented-farmland-for-2026.html)

**Counterexample / limit.** Rent alone does not dictate crop. Soybeans can win on a field's yields, input bill, basis, rotational effects or price; the rent paper is identical whichever crop the tenant plants. Survey averages also conceal field quality and sampling variation. (read; farmdoc, 2026-09; https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2026/09/illinois-cash-rents-in-2026-and-outlook-for-2027.html)

### Tenure mix and fixed-rent risk

**Mechanism.** On cash-rented acres, the tenant retains crop revenue but owes rent through a bad yield/price year. On crop-share acres, landowner and tenant divide output and usually specified variable costs; the contract can align both sides around long-run land productivity. USDA's 39% rented figure is all farmland, including pasture and woodland, not cropland alone; ERS reports that more than half of US cropland is rented. Illinois commercial grain farms enrolled in FBFM averaged 50% cash-rented acres in 2024, and cash-rent share increased from 46% in 2020 as crop share declined from 30% to 26%. (read; ERS 2024-05; farmdoc 2026-01; https://ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/109182 ; https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2026/01/tenure-characteristics-of-illinois-farmland-6.html)

**Numbers and who pays.** The cited 2026 Illinois budget returned −$15/ac on a cash-rented acre versus +$18/ac under modeled 50/50 share rent for the same crop mix. The tenant/operator pays the set rent and operating bills on cash rent; in share leases, the lease defines each party's portion of income and costs. (read; farmdoc, 2026-01; same URL above)

**Counterexample / limit.** Tenure is not a sufficient predictor of crop practice. The same Illinois source is a commercial-grain-farm sample, not a representative estimate for every farm; ERS total farmland rented is only 39%, not the roughly half sometimes repeated. (read; ERS 2024-05; farmdoc 2026-01; URLs above)

### Lease duration, landlord terms, and decision authority

**Mechanism.** A short lease exposes tenants to losing the land before a multi-year small-grain, cover-crop or alfalfa sequence pays back. A written lease can specify crop or practice rules; a relationship can also enable a rotation change. In Practical Farmers of Iowa's case, Paul Thompson and Elena Meyer selected tenant Josh Nelson to add no-till, covers and a third crop; they offered a significant discount on up to 30 acres of small grain because of its financial risk. That is a documented example, not an estimate of how often landlords dictate rotations. (read; PFI, case history published online, corrected 2024-05-24; https://practicalfarmers.org/resources/articles/finding-common-ground)

**Numbers and who pays.** A lease discount on up to 30 small-grain acres shares transition risk with the owner. USDA 2024 TOTAL found 2.09 million landlords rented out 347.8 million acres; 87% of landlords were non-operating and principal landlord age averaged 69.2 years. The tenant still decides within the lease terms and needs an agreement to lock in longer-term practice. (read; USDA-NASS 2026-03; https://nass.usda.gov/Publications/Highlights/2026/TOTAL24.pdf; lease-term guidance (read), Mississippi State Extension, date not stated: https://extension.msstate.edu/sites/default/files/publications/P4091_web.pdf)

**Counterexample / limit.** A landlord is not necessarily a farm manager, and a lease restriction is not automatic. UCANR describes cash-rent tenants as usually having operating freedom subject to lease restrictions; crop-share leases involve more joint decisions. Multi-generation / relationship authority is real but needs field-level interviews; population landlord age does not tell us who approves a crop change. (read; UCANR, undated; USDA-NASS 2026-03; URLs above)

### Base acres and commodity-program payments

**Mechanism.** ARC/PLC payments tied to base acreage were historically decoupled from current planted crop, so legacy base should not be represented as a direct planting constraint. For 2026 the regulation created up to 30 million additional base acres based on 2019-23 planting/prevented-plant history, with commodity allocation linked to crop history. FSA says allocation completed with 3.69% pro-rata reduction. Landowners reviewed notices; eligible operators then made annual ARC/PLC elections. This is a one-time program change, not an annual crop reward. (read; eCFR 2026-01; FSA 2026-09-15; https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-7/subtitle-B/chapter-XIV/subchapter-B/part-1412/subpart-B/section-1412.27 ; https://fsa.usda.gov/news-events/news/09-15-2026/usda-announces-2026-2027-enrollment-key-price-revenue-safety-net)

**Numbers and who pays.** The national ceiling was 30 million new acres; eligible totals exceeded that cap, producing a 3.69% reduction. Farm-level overlap among insured and base acres cannot be calculated publicly because the required farm-operation matching data are unavailable. (read; FSA 2026-09; farmdoc 2026-08; https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2026/08/insured-acres-base-acres-and-the-farm-bills-crop-risk-assistance.html)

**Counterexample / limit.** Crop-specific base and planted crop need not match. The budget should include only a farm's actual base, yield and election; national averages are not a planting incentive estimate. The new acres reflect 2019-23 activity but were allocated for 2026 and later program payments, so do not infer a farmer's 2026 crop is changed by the same planting that established their historical record. (read; eCFR 2026-01; FSA 2026-09; URLs above)

### One-time planted-acre bridge aid

**Mechanism.** FBA can show up in a crop-year cash flow, but it was a one-time 2025-crop support payment based on planted acres, not a recurring expected gross margin. In 2025, corn's rate exceeded soy's, which can affect retrospective accounting if the acres were already planted; future rotation analysis must zero it out unless a successor program is explicitly documented. (read; USDA 2025-12-31; https://usda.gov/about-usda/news/press-releases/2025/12/31/usda-announces-commodity-payment-rates-farmer-bridge-assistance-program)

**Numbers and who pays.** The rates were $44.36/ac for corn and $30.88/ac for soybeans, a $13.48/ac difference, paid against 2025 planted acres. Under share rent, the distribution follows the lease; farmdoc estimated a 50/50 corn-soy 2025 rotation's average FBA at $37.62/planted acre. (read; USDA 2025-12-31; farmdoc 2026-01; https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2026/01/farmer-bridge-assistance-program-payment-rates.html)

**Counterexample / limit.** Payment is backward-looking and the $13.48/ac difference is not a recurring price ratio or agronomic benefit. It also excludes prevent-plant acres, and eligibility rules matter. (read; USDA 2025-12-31; same USDA URL above)

### Crop insurance guarantee and own-crop history

**Mechanism.** Insurance is a crop-specific revenue and yield floor derived from the projected price, coverage, yield history, and county/unit rules. Rotation decisions affect expected yields and loss distributions, but a corn APH should not be treated as soybean APH. The 2026 projected price was $4.62/bu for corn and $11.09/bu for soybeans; these values set price inputs to guarantees and premiums, not a guaranteed farm payment. RMA provisions define APH using a minimum of four and maximum of ten consecutive crop years when available. (read; farmdoc 2026-03; RMA 2026-06; https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2026/03/projected-prices-and-volatility-factors-for-2026.html ; https://rma.usda.gov/sites/default/files/2026-06/Common%20Crop%20Insurance%20Policy%20Basic%20Provisions%2027.1-BR.pdf)

**Numbers and who pays.** Corn's 2026 projected price was 8¢/bu below 2025's $4.70, while soybeans' $11.09 was 55¢/bu above 2025's $10.54. Producer premium depends on coverage and risk characteristics; operator pays the premium, lender may require adequate coverage for credit, and indemnities depend on insured loss. (read; farmdoc 2026-03; same farmdoc URL above)

**Counterexample / limit.** Insurance prices alone cannot say which crop is preferable: yield, premium subsidy, APH, crop loss correlation and farm-level guarantees matter. Also, available coverage can differ by product, but do not assume insurance forces planting a matching base crop. (read; farmdoc 2026-03; RMA 2026-06; URLs above)

### Futures price and corn/soy price ratio

**Mechanism.** Expected relative crop price is a visible signal, but farmers describe the operational ability to shift acres as constrained by rotation and agronomy. In Farm Futures' March 2025 survey of its respondents, 75% held rotations for agronomic reasons, 38% could change less than one-quarter of acres, and only 13% had changed intentions due to recent price trends. These are survey respondents, not all US farmers. (read; Farm Futures article for survey closed 2025-03-18; https://www.farmprogress.com/planting/farmers-stick-to-crop-rotations-despite-market-signals-farm-futures-survey-shows)

**Numbers and who pays.** Ryan Facemire, Franklin, Indiana, said at the third week of March 2026 local fall-delivery bids were $4.52/bu corn and $11/bu soy, and his marginal corn needed 190 bu/ac to break even versus a 40-bu/ac soybean break-even; he switched all acres to soybeans. His quoted thresholds are operation-specific, not national crop budgets. (read; Farm Progress, 2026-10; https://www.farmprogress.com/management/tale-of-two-farmers-nitrogen-prices-force-strategy-rewrite; corroborating interview: https://www.farmprogress.com/soybean/don-t-short-second-year-soybeans)

**Counterexample / limit.** The 2025 survey also reported that most respondents maintained rotations despite price signals, and Facemire's response is an unusually large crop switch that depended on his own cost/break-even picture. Do not use a single national corn/soy ratio threshold as an acreage forecast. (read; Farm Futures 2025-03; Facemire 2026-10; URLs above)

### Local elevator / processor basis and delivery

**Mechanism.** Farmer revenue is local cash bid, not futures alone: basis, delivery timing, storage, drying and trucking matter. DTN's 2026-11-04 report showed national average basis at 36¢ under Dec corn and 64¢ under Jan soy; it also listed corn Decatur IL at +10¢ Z versus Fort Dodge IA at −40¢ Z and soy Decatur IL +6¢ F versus Grand Island NE −100¢ F. Those local spreads demonstrate why a rotation tool needs buyer-level bids. (read; DTN 2026-11-04; https://dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/news/article/2026/11/04/corn-soybean-basis-stronger)

**Numbers and who pays.** At the source's 2026-11-04 snapshot, basis swings of 50¢/bu corn and 106¢/bu soy separated those cited locations, before hauling, quality, and contract terms. The producer realizes the local bid; landlord share depends on the lease, and the elevator or processor sets a location-specific bid. (read; DTN 2026-11-04; same URL above)

**Counterexample / limit.** One day of bids is not a seasonal or forward-price forecast. A stronger basis at one buyer may be offset by haul distance, freight, grade or a different delivery window; compare actual farm-net bids. (read; DTN 2026-11-04; same URL above)

### Nitrogen/fertilizer cost and sunk preplant inputs

**Mechanism.** Corn commonly carries a large nitrogen bill, and fertilizer bought/applied before a price change is sunk for a late switch. This can lock fields into corn even when soy looks more attractive, but continuous soy requires its own potassium and fertility management. In 2026 Facemire cited steep N costs and switched after determining corn break-even on his marginal acres; Brian Scott said sidedress nitrogen became his highest corn cost besides land and was about $50/ac above his spring budget. (read; Farm Progress, 2026-10; https://www.farmprogress.com/management/tale-of-two-farmers-nitrogen-prices-force-strategy-rewrite)

**Numbers and who pays.** Facemire said the crop change reduced his input outlay by $350,000 across the operation; he then bought another combine as a hedge against harvesting an all-soybean crop. Scott priced two 30,000-gallon nitrogen tanks, with full infrastructure investment around $160,000, after estimating $70,000 savings at a summer nitrogen price. Operator pays and carries risk; the retailer's bid/timing matters. (read; Farm Progress, 2026-10; same URL above)

**Counterexample / limit.** Facemire said soybeans would still require attention to potassium; continuous beans were not a zero-input alternative. Scott chose storage and kept corn rather than switching crop, showing the same cost shock can produce different adaptations. These are two reported cases, not a national fertilizer elasticity. (read; Farm Progress, 2026-10; https://www.farmprogress.com/soybean/don-t-short-second-year-soybeans)

### Fuel, tillage and other variable costs

**Mechanism.** Fuel price can make tillage, drying and harvest choices more important. Farmers may first cut passes instead of changing crop, while reduced tillage is also affected by soil moisture and weed-management needs. Northeast Iowa farmer Mark Mueller said he paid $2.29/gal diesel in May 2025 and about $5/gal for a 2026 harvest purchase; Ryan Vos in southwest Minnesota said he planned to till bean ground less under dry, hard conditions and higher fuel prices. (read; AgWeb, 2026-10; https://agweb.com/news/business/triple-threat-fuel-fertilizer-financing-compress-farm-margins)

**Numbers and who pays.** The reported diesel increase was about $2.71/gal between those two purchase observations, not a national price series. Operator pays fuel directly and may alter field passes; crop-share leases may specify how some costs are shared. (read; AgWeb, 2026-10; same URL above)

**Counterexample / limit.** Vos also said he was reluctant to cut fertilizer because yield loss could cost more. Fuel pressure may change tillage or purchasing strategy without changing crop rotation. (read; AgWeb, 2026-10; same URL above)

### Equipment width, field days and timeliness

**Mechanism.** Planting and harvest capacity put hard limits on acres and sequence. Iowa State's February 2025 update used NASS field-day records from 1964-2024: northwest Iowa median 12.6 suitable days from Apr 30-May 20; 1,600 acres of corn require 127 acres/day or 9.1 acres/hour over a 14-hour day. Spring days declined from a trend estimate of 46 to 37 since 1964. A larger machine can reduce timing risk but raises ownership cost. (read; Iowa State, 2025-02; https://extension.iastate.edu/agdm/crops/html/a3-25.html)

**Numbers and who pays.** In 2024, Iowa reported 35.4 suitable spring field days against 41.3 average since 1964. The farmer/operator carries machine capital, labor and custom-operation costs; the rotation may need to fit planter, drill, header, storage and harvest windows. (read; Iowa State, 2025-02; same URL above)

**Counterexample / limit.** The Iowa example is a capacity illustration, not proof that farms need wider machinery or that small grains are impossible. The same extension lists outsourcing, fewer passes and efficiency technologies as alternatives. (read; Iowa State, 2025-02; same URL above)

### Harvest labor, storage and crop logistics

**Mechanism.** Harvest labor and material flow can constrain crop choice and acres. Mike Brelsford, a 6,000-acre Iowa farmer, said his 36-row planter and combine system lets him plant in about two weeks and harvest in six to eight weeks; he said a second combine would require eight workers and finding eight at harvest was “virtually impossible.” His statement is direct farmer voice but an individual farm case. (read; Wallace Farmer, 2025-12; https://www.qgdigitalpublishing.com/article/Should+you+skinny+down+your+line%3F/5079686/857804/article.html)

**Numbers and who pays.** His combine was described as moving 6,000 bu/hour, with a grain cart dump about every 550 bu. A different 2026 case, Facemire, reported soybean harvest fuel of about 1 gal/ac versus 1.8 for corn and roughly 100 fewer elevator loads; he bought an additional combine after changing to all soybeans. Operator bears labor, machinery, storage and delivery costs. (read; Wallace Farmer 2025-12; Farm Progress 2026-10; URLs above)

**Counterexample / limit.** The equipment response is not simply “soybeans need less capacity”: Facemire added a combine to manage harvest risk, and on-farm storage or carts can substitute for labor. One large-farm profile does not estimate a regional labor shortage. (read; Farm Progress 2026-10; Wallace Farmer 2025-12; URLs above)

### Rotational yield and input credit

**Mechanism.** Rotational partners can affect yield and expenses over multiple years. An analysis of 748,374 Midwest field-level records from 2007-12 estimated a 4.3% rainfed continuous-corn yield penalty, a 10.3% continuous-soybean penalty, and a 1.7% continuous-corn penalty estimate under irrigation. This is a historical dataset; water, soil, climate and field effects matter. (read; Agronomy Journal, 2016; https://acsess.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.2134/agronj2016.03.0134)

**Numbers and who pays.** UMN's 2026 extension summary cites Illinois research with 7% second-year and 10% continuous-corn penalties versus corn after soybeans. Corn after soybean can also benefit from N credits. The operator bears realized yield/cost effects; owner/share landlord shares them only under lease terms. (read; UMN Extension, 2026-04; https://blog-crop-news.extension.umn.edu/2026/04/strategic-farming-planting-2026-crop.html; Wallaces Farmer, 2026-03-30: https://www.farmprogress.com/crops/why-corn-and-soybeans-form-a-powerful-tag-team)

**Counterexample / limit.** Average penalties conceal field-level variation; the 2016 analysis estimated only 1.7% for irrigated continuous corn after environmental controls. A yield penalty alone does not determine profit if corn's price, costs, market access or water are different. (read; Agronomy Journal, 2016; URL above)

### Herbicide resistance and weed seedbank

**Mechanism.** Resistant waterhemp and Palmer can reduce the reliability of crop-specific herbicide programs, raising scouting, residual, mechanical control or seedbank costs. Crop rotation changes emergence timing and available control tactics; it cannot substitute for an effective multi-mode program. The 2026 Illinois study examined four suspected glufosinate-resistant waterhemp populations and confirmed at one field site that 20% survived the labeled rate. (read; Weed Science, DOI 10.1017/wsc.2026.10111; https://doi.org/10.1017/wsc.2026.10111)

**Numbers and who pays.** Wisconsin research reported soybean canopy in 15-inch rows closing about 10 days earlier than 30-inch rows, improving late-season suppression opportunity. A 2025 USAFRI summary reported established alfalfa reduced waterhemp seed production to under 50 seeds/plant at low stand density, versus as many as 250,000 in corn/soy systems in the cited research. Farmer pays for herbicide, scouting, crop choice and any yield loss; adviser can influence timely application. (read; Farm Progress 2026-04; USAFRI 2025; https://www.farmprogress.com/weeds/waterhemp-stages-a-chemical-coup-on-ppo-and-dicamba ; https://alfalfa.org/pdf/USAFRI/2025/25Kezar.pdf)

**Counterexample / limit.** Troy Monaghan near Manchester, Iowa, reported that his 2025 corn program controlled waterhemp with one post pass until canopy and avoided a second spray; the story was submitted by Corteva and should be flagged as sponsored. Also, resistance is population/field-specific; an assay or local weed-control history is needed before recommending a rotation. (read; Farm Progress/Corteva-submitted, 2025-12-01; https://www.farmprogress.com/crop-protection/young-grower-s-strategic-weed-control-plan-vanquishes-tough-waterhemp-in-corn)

### Cover crops / EQIP and CSP incentives

**Mechanism.** EQIP/CSP can reduce the farmer's net cost of a cover crop or a longer rotation, but practice enrollment, contract length, payment and establishment/termination costs vary. For FY2026 Kansas CSP, the schedule lists $10.48/ac for basic two-crop rotation, $120.67/ac for an improved resource-conserving rotation, $125.15/ac for single-species cover and $130.49/ac for multispecies cover. These are scheduled unit costs, not guaranteed payments to every applicant. (read; NRCS Kansas FY2026 schedule, version 2026-03-02; https://nrcs.usda.gov/sites/default/files/2026-03/FY26_CSP_PaymentSchedule.pdf)

**Numbers and who pays.** National Census data recorded cover crops on 4.7% of cropland in 2022; Purdue's 2025 survey summary estimated about 22% adoption among its Ag Economy Barometer respondents, a different and more selected survey population. Operator bears establishment and management work; NRCS payments offset eligible contracted practices. (read; Purdue, 2025-10; https://ag.purdue.edu/commercialag/home/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/20251029_CoverCropAdoption.pdf)

**Counterexample / limit.** The payment rates do not equal farmer profit and may not cover foregone crop income or operating risk. The 2025 survey synthesis reports concerns about profitability and yield impact; some past users discontinue cover crops. (read; Purdue, 2025-10; same URL above)

### Agronomist / retailer recommendation and service

**Mechanism.** Advisers can shape a rotation or its execution through local yield history, scouting, input selection, seed, custom application and operational logistics. Purdue's 2025 Large Commercial Producer Survey of 1,151 large commercial producers, as reported by AGDAILY, found the top new-idea source was ag media (25.6%), followed by other farmers (21%) and retailer/dealer resources (13.6%). Retailers may still influence implementation more than idea discovery. (read; AGDAILY, 2026-07; https://agdaily.com/news/farmers-trust-ag-media-peers-exploring-new-practices)

**Numbers and who pays.** Monaghan said his retailer delivered “hot loads” to the field so he could spray alone without a mixer/tender. That is a concrete service that saves labor on one Iowa farm, but no dollars/hour were reported. Agronomist/retailer is usually a paid or product-linked adviser; operator pays for the service/input. (read; Farm Progress/Corteva-submitted, 2025-12-01; https://www.farmprogress.com/crop-protection/young-grower-s-strategic-weed-control-plan-vanquishes-tough-waterhemp-in-corn)

**Counterexample / limit.** Retailer/dealer sources ranked first for only 13.6% in the Purdue survey, less than ag media or peers. Vendor stories and vendor interpretation of survey data should not be treated as neutral evidence of effectiveness. (read; AGDAILY 2026-07; Farm Progress 2025-12; URLs above)

### Ogallala availability and Kansas LEMA allocation

**Mechanism.** Water allocation changes both irrigation quantity per acre and the relative value of water-intensive crops. In the 2013 Sheridan County 6 LEMA, irrigation was limited to 55 inches per eligible acre over five years. The 2016 K-State analysis estimated irrigated corn acres fell 23%, sorghum acres rose 68%, soybean acres rose 8%, and all irrigated crop acres fell 5%; study authors note sorghum acreage began from a small base. A 2026 difference-in-differences analysis estimated SD-6 water-use intensity fell 16% and pumping costs $5.48/ac, with no significant irrigated-area change. (read; K-State 2016; JARE 2026; https://accc.k-state.edu/research/factsheets/FS7_LEMA.pdf ; https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/358997/files/JARE358997.pdf)

**Numbers and who pays.** KGS measured a provisional 0.2-ft overall Kansas High Plains aquifer level increase in 2025 after five years of declines; the 1996-2025 annual average was −0.57 ft, and GMD3 declined 0.62 ft in 2025. Irrigator/operator pays pumping cost and takes yield risk; a right-holder faces the binding allocation; lender is exposed to water-right and collateral value risk. (read; KGS, 2026; https://kgs.ku.edu/news/article/groundwater-levels-in-the-kansas-high-plains-aquifer-see-first-overall-increase-since-2019)

**Counterexample / limit.** Weather drove the 2025 aquifer-wide increase, including localized May-June rainfall up to 12 inches. The newer JARE study finds a reduction in applied-water intensity without a significant acreage response. A water cap's effect depends on whether it binds, and results from one LEMA should not be copied to all Ogallala farms. (read; KGS 2026; JARE 2026; URLs above)

### California SGMA, surface-water reliability and fallowing

**Mechanism.** SGMA requires basins to reach sustainability by the 2040s through demand reduction and/or new supplies. The local rotation effect depends on groundwater allocation and surface-water delivery; orchard removal, annual crop substitution, water trading and fallowing have different cost structures. PPIC's scenarios estimate water supplies could fall 20% (about 3.2 MAF), with 500,000-900,000 acres potentially fallowed by 2040 depending on water supply and trading. Those are projections, not observed SGMA-caused acres. (read; PPIC 2023; https://www.ppic.org/publication/policy-brief-the-future-of-agriculture-in-the-san-joaquin-valley/)

**Numbers and who pays.** PPIC's 2026 update estimates Central Valley overdraft at about 1.1 MAF/yr for 2021-25 versus 1.9 MAF/yr in 2003-10, while 45% of monitoring wells showed declines over the past two decades. Westlands reported more than 215,000 acres out of production in 2025, but attributed this to unreliable/inadequate water supplies and SGMA together; the reported figure is not a validated SGMA-only estimate. Growers/landowners lose crop revenue or bear pumping fees, fallow management and land repurposing costs. (read; PPIC 2026-09; Westlands Water District 2025-12-16; https://ppic.org/blog/california-is-finally-addressing-groundwater-overdraft-how-is-it-going ; https://wwd.ca.gov/wwd-media/press-release-12-16-2025)

**Counterexample / limit.** PPIC reports 775,000 acre-feet/year in anticipated benefits from completed projects and substantial recharge progress after wet 2023; surface supplies and water trading can reduce fallowing need or shift scarcity costs. There is no agency-validated SGMA-attributable fallowed-acre total for 2024-26 in the sources found here. (read; PPIC 2026-09; source above)

### Forage / alfalfa value and water productivity

**Mechanism.** Alfalfa and other forage are multi-year, water-intensive crops whose value comes from multiple cuts, quality, livestock/dairy demand and stand persistence. They cannot be evaluated like an annual grain crop: establishment, contract, water and land opportunity cost run across years. USDA-reported September 2026 national alfalfa price was $208/ton, up from $172/ton a year earlier; state averages varied widely, from Nebraska $106 to Colorado $245/ton. (read; USDA-NASS Agricultural Prices, 2026-10-30; values accessible from https://agsist.com/hay-prices)

**Numbers and who pays.** UNL's 2026 gravity-irrigated alfalfa budget used 6.6 tons/ac and calculated $892.39/ac total economic cost ($135.21/ton). At $106/ton Nebraska state-average alfalfa price, gross revenue would be $699.60/ac before accounting for the fact that budget costs/yields are a specific enterprise scenario. Water and stand establishment costs bind the tenant/operator; forage buyers and livestock operators determine local value. (read; UNL 2026 budgets, revised 2025-10; https://cap.unl.edu/sites/unl.edu.ianr.agecon.center-for-ag-profitability/files/media/file/2026-nebraska-crop-budgets.pdf)

**Counterexample / limit.** NASS state price is an all-quality average and not a dairy-grade contract. An older UC Davis cost-study index does not provide a 2025-26 California enterprise budget in the evidence gathered; water use and net margin must be customized locally. Price rebound alone does not establish acreage response. (read; NASS 2026-10; UC Davis alfalfa study index: https://coststudies.ucdavis.edu/current/commodity/alfalfa)

### Frost, freeze and growing-season window

**Mechanism.** Frost changes crop-stage risk and double-crop timing, not simply the region's calendar. K-State reports March 15-16, 2026 Kansas minima ranged from −1°F in northwest Kansas to 17°F south-central. Estimated injurious exposure to wheat at tillering/jointing stages is around two hours at 12°F/24°F respectively, with field conditions changing injury; extension advises waiting 10-14 warm days to assess. (read; K-State Agronomy eUpdate 2026-03-19; https://eupdate.agronomy.ksu.edu/article/wheat-status-injury-symptoms-from-freeze-damage-687-4)

**Numbers and who pays.** Freeze risk affects wheat yield, harvest decisions, crop insurance and next-crop window; a farmer may abandon or replant only after assessment. K-State notes variety dormancy release can differ by as much as three weeks. Operator bears uninsured yield loss and replant costs; insurer pays only if policy and cause-of-loss conditions are met. (read; K-State, 2026-03-19; same URL above)

**Counterexample / limit.** The same source emphasizes injury depends on stage, stand density, residue, soil moisture, wind and field position; a regional temperature alone is not a yield loss estimate. Drought-stressed plants can sometimes be more cold tolerant, further complicating a single “freeze penalty.” (read; K-State, 2026-03-19; same URL above)

### Local crop market, feed, processing and contracts

**Mechanism.** A crop may fit a system because it has a nearby buyer, a livestock use or a rotational residue value. Mark Licht of Iowa State described corn-soybeans as equipment-compatible and supported by livestock/biofuel markets, while saying small grains have smaller markets. Hoxie, Kansas farmer Brett Oelke said wheat residue conserves soil moisture for following corn and he had few cash-crop alternatives with similar residue. (read; Wallaces Farmer 2026-03-30; DTN 2026-03-13; https://www.farmprogress.com/crops/why-corn-and-soybeans-form-a-powerful-tag-team ; https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/crops/article/2026/03/13/high-input-costs-low-prices-squeeze)

**Numbers and who pays.** Oelke said he aims to trim $20-$30/ac nitrogen cost without sacrificing a bushel and described mill relationships that add value for wheat. Iowa plantings are roughly 10 million acres soy and 13-14 million acres corn (article's statewide context). Producer bears market, contract and switching costs; livestock operator may value feed locally. (read; DTN 2026-03-13; Wallaces Farmer 2026-03-30; URLs above)

**Counterexample / limit.** A farmer may want a third crop but lack a market; an intended wheat/soy double crop can fail when rain delays wheat harvest. David Walton, eastern Iowa, described leaving intended double-crop soybeans unplanted after delayed wheat harvest in 2024. (read; Farm Progress, 2026-03-30; https://farmprogress.com/wheat/why-winter-wheat-deserves-a-second-look-in-iowa?index=)

## Region notes

### Corn Belt: Iowa, Illinois, Nebraska, Minnesota

- **Land and lease exposure differ inside the belt.** Illinois's 2026 statewide cash rent was $261/ac, and the 2026 central high-productivity example was $327/ac; Iowa's 2026 average was $270/crop acre in the Iowa State survey. Do not project those high-yield rents to Nebraska or Minnesota without local county/field data. (read; farmdoc 2026-09; Iowa State 2026-05; https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2026/09/illinois-cash-rents-in-2026-and-outlook-for-2027.html ; https://agrimarketing.com/s/157140)
- **Annual corn-soy is structurally supported** by compatible equipment, extensive grain buyers, feed/ethanol/crush, and crop sequence benefits. Survey evidence nevertheless shows limited ability or intent to move whole farms rapidly from the rotation; some farmers may only flex marginal acres. (read; Farm Futures 2025-03; Wallaces Farmer 2026-03-30; URLs in Source list)
- **Weed resistance is a direct rotation pressure.** Waterhemp resistance has reached multiple modes of action in parts of the Corn Belt. A local seedbank/resistance history should inform rotation and spray-plan alternatives; product choice is not a substitute for resistance testing and integrated management. (read; Weed Science 2026; Farm Progress 2026-04)
- **Equipment and harvest operations can dominate.** In NW Iowa's 2025 field-day example, 1,600 acres need 9.1 acres/hour in a 12.6-day planting window. Crop and field sequence should be checked against the actual planter, combine, storage and labor system. (read; Iowa State 2025-02)
- **Nebraska spans Corn Belt and High Plains drivers.** Eastern Nebraska shares corn-soy markets, while the western part faces irrigation allocation, aquifer decline and alfalfa/forage tradeoffs; one statewide note would hide that difference. (read; KGS 2026; UNL 2026 budgets)

### Plains and irrigated West: Kansas, Nebraska, Texas, California

- **Water rights and pumping constraints can take precedence over futures.** Kansas LEMAs have demonstrated a crop-mix adjustment in SD-6, but the more recent evaluation finds lower water intensity without a significant irrigated-acreage change. In Nebraska/Texas, measure well-level water, pumping cost and locally applicable rules rather than importing a Kansas response. (read; K-State 2016; JARE 2026; KGS 2026)
- **Sorghum, wheat and dryland rotations can be water/soil adaptations, not simply lower-value substitutes.** In western Kansas, Brett Oelke says wheat residue is important to retain moisture for corn; fall frost and double-crop feasibility shape the following crop. (read; DTN 2026-03-13; K-State 2026-03-19)
- **Irrigated forage economics are location specific.** Nebraska's 2026 budget estimated gravity-irrigated alfalfa economic cost of $892/ac; September 2026 NASS alfalfa price ranged from $106/ton in Nebraska to $245/ton in Colorado. A high hay price does not settle margin without yield, grade, water fee and delivery. (read; UNL 2026 budget; NASS 2026-10-30)
- **California has both surface-water reliability and groundwater management constraints.** SGMA basin status and GSA pumping rules are local; PPIC fallowing numbers are 2040 scenarios, while Westlands' 2025 acreage-out-of-production figure includes multiple water-supply causes. Treat almond/orchard capital and land repurposing separately from annual grain fields. (read; DWR 2026-10; PPIC 2023/2026; Westlands 2025-12-16)
- **Frost risk is crop-stage and microclimate specific.** Kansas March 2026 minimums varied by 18°F across the reported NW to south-central locations; Kansas wheat stage, variety, residue and field position changed injury risk. Use county/field history and insurance, not a single Plains-wide freeze-risk value. (read; K-State 2026-03-19)

## Tenant and landlord split

**Scale and correction.** USDA ERS reports 39% of all US farmland was rented in 2022, while more than half of cropland was rented; the brief's “roughly half of US farmland” should not be repeated without specifying cropland. In 2024, USDA-NASS counted 347.8 million rented acres and found 87% of landlords were non-operating. Non-operating principal landlords averaged 69.2 years old, and 52% had never farmed. These data indicate a fragmented, often non-operating ownership side, not a single “landlord” decision-maker. (read; ERS 2024-05; USDA-NASS 2026-03; https://ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/109182 ; https://nass.usda.gov/Publications/Highlights/2026/TOTAL24.pdf)

**Cash rent.** Tenant/operator keeps the crop upside but faces the fixed rent obligation and most production/price risk. For the 2026 central Illinois budget, the projected cash-rent result was −$15/ac versus +$232/ac debt-free owned and +$18/ac share-rented under the same 50/50 corn-soy budget. That is a local model, not a national farm result. (read; farmdoc 2026-01; https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2026/01/projected-incomes-on-owned-vs-rented-farmland-for-2026.html)

**Crop share / flexible rent.** Landowner and tenant can share yield, price and input costs, but the contract must say which inputs and crops are shared. Extension guidance says crop-share agreements require joint decisions and detailed negotiation; a lease template can specify crop shares and general cropping system. (read; Oklahoma State Extension, undated, https://extension.okstate.edu/fact-sheets/print-publications/agec/developing-share-lease-agreements-for-farmland-agec-215.pdf; Illinois crop-share form, https://farmdoc.illinois.edu/assets/legal/form/Crop_Share_Lease.pdf)

**Landlord authority / multigeneration.** A lease binds both parties; crop restrictions or conservation provisions can be negotiated. The PFI Iowa example shows landlords selecting a tenant aligned to a third-crop objective and subsidizing transition acreage. A separate 2026 farmer interview, Donald Maring of Maryland, describes decades-long landlord relationships and lease history through his father, as well as competition from other tenants. That illustrates relationship and succession effects, not a national rule. (read; PFI case corrected 2024-05-24; Farm Progress, 2026-11; URLs above)

**What the tool should ask each side.** Tenant: rent per acre and payment schedule; length and termination date; existing prepaid inputs; what yields and crop histories transfer; who pays for fertility, cover crop, irrigation, drainage and custom work; whether the crop change jeopardizes a buyer or loan. Owner/landlord: written crop/soil requirements; lease renewability; soil and herbicide history; who owns FSA base and who can provide permission/data; how a perennial crop or conservation investment is compensated. Agronomist/manager: confirm contract conditions and field-specific spray, nutrient, water and harvest feasibility before recommending a switch. These are operational questions inferred from cited lease guidance and farmer cases, not claims that every lease contains these terms. (read; Mississippi State Extension; MSU leasing checklist reported in Farm Progress; URLs above)

## What I could not find

- A recent, nationally representative causal estimate of how a $1/ac increase in cash rent changes a farm's corn/soy/third-crop acreage. Local budgets show margins; operator interviews and acreage surveys show mechanisms, not causal elasticities.
- Public farm-operation-level data matching individual RMA insured acres, APH, FSA base acres, owner/tenant shares and current planted acres. Farmdoc explicitly says this matching question cannot be answered with public data. (read; farmdoc, 2026-08; https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2026/08/insured-acres-base-acres-and-the-farm-bills-crop-risk-assistance.html)
- A validated 2024-26 count of California acres fallowed specifically because of SGMA, separated from surface-water allocations, drought, prices, orchard removal and other causes. Westlands reports acres out of production but combines multiple water constraints.
- A current, comparable 2025-26 crop budget for alfalfa across California, Idaho and the Plains that standardizes water cost, forage grade, stand age, land rent and harvest/storage. Nebraska has a 2026 budget, but it is not portable to every irrigated West district.
- A nationally representative current measure of how often landlords formally approve crop rotations, how many leases are written multi-year, or how frequently landlord pressure blocks a tenant's crop choice. The available examples are case studies and extension guidance.
- Field-level choice data that isolate retailer/agronomist advice as the cause of a crop rotation change from grower experience, input sales, financing or buyer contracts.
- A single value for labour scarcity or equipment-width constraint that generalizes across farm size and region. The evidence includes a local Iowa suitable-day calculation and individual grower accounts, not national rotation elasticities.
- A clean, nationally comparable 2026 set of realized crop prices, local basis, fertilizer prices and insurance outcomes by farm. DTN basis is a dated market snapshot; enterprise budgets are projections and must be replaced with the user's own bids and costs.
