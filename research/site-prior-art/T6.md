## What the site implies about the intended solution

- **Inference — the deliverable is a decision-support tool, not a satellite-data viewer.** Observed basis: the challenge summary says combine NASA Earth observations with local soil, crop and farmer priorities to choose rotations that protect soil and conserve water; the page warns local soil/crop data are not NASA products and suggests sourcing them from a partner or public soil survey.[3]
- **Inference — the minimum useful prototype is a field-level rotation comparison.** Observed basis: the first hour says compare at least three rotations, including the current rotation, and show evidence and risks for each. This is stronger than merely plotting moisture or vegetation.[3]
- **Inference — water stress and crop history are high-priority signals.** Observed basis: SMAP is listed first among suggested datasets; then ECOSTRESS and GPM; the first-hour sequence calls for all three resampled to one common grid. HLS gives finer, frequent surface-reflectance observations; GRACE is a monthly, broad-scale total-water-storage context layer, not an individual-field sensor.[3]
- **Inference — the page’s practical starting measurement is local weather/climate, not SMAP.** Observed basis: step one is to run POWER for the farm coordinates for rainfall, temperature and sunlight, before the second step adds SMAP, ECOSTRESS and GPM.[3]
- **Inference — five products carry an access cost in the form of account setup/download friction.** Observed basis: five of six cards display Earthdata Login; searching can be public, but downloads require a free account. POWER has no login and browser access. “Cost” here means onboarding/time and not a paid subscription.[3][5]
- **Inference — a credible prototype should normalize grids and expose limits.** Observed basis: the first-hour instruction says resample disparate observations to one common grid; card copy gives product resolution and timing caveats, including GPM Final’s lag and GRACE’s monthly scale.[3]
- **Inference — the author expects an explainable, farmer-facing workflow, not coverage of every crop or region.** Observed basis: one concrete “farm’s coordinates” start, three comparisons, and risks/evidence per rotation. The site never says to build an all-crops global optimizer.[3]
- **Inference — scope should be a usable slice rather than an exhaustive system.** Observed basis: its 2025 matched examples include a farming-education mini-game and a locally focused water/ecosystem restoration project; those are distinct scopes, not evidence that either provides rotation scoring.[3][12]
- **Inference — the Atlas is designed to shorten time-to-first-query and support AI-assisted building.** Observed basis: copy/paste starters, Colab links, the setup checklist, and a free read-only MCP server offering dataset and live CMR lookup.[4][5][7]

## Starter code review

**Result: zero of the seven challenge-page code snippets failed in the tested environment (read).** I extracted and ran the six Python examples (SMAP, HLS, GRACE, POWER, GPM and ECOSTRESS) using `earthaccess 0.19.0` and `requests`, and ran the JavaScript POWER example with Node 26.12.0. All completed successfully: NASA searches returned records (5, 5, 1, 5 and 5 for the five search snippets), the POWER Python example returned 153 days and 621.8 mm for its specified 2025 period, and the JavaScript example returned seven daily temperatures. These are reproducibility checks at the access/search layer, not validation of agronomic meaning or file contents.[3][4]

The live widget’s actual implementation in `assets/site.js` queries `https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/granules.json` with `collection_concept_id=C2938664763-NSIDC_CPRD`, `sort_key=-start_date`, `page_size=5`, and `Client-Id: space-data-atlas`; it prints each granule ID and date. A direct `curl` of that exact request returned HTTP 200, `CMR-Hits: 4159`, and five newest SMAP granules dated 2026-11-08 through 2026-11-04. The widget therefore works as a public catalogue search and does not download or decode any HDF5 science data.[9][10]

What a reader still has to supply:

- Python dependencies (`earthaccess`, `requests`, and, for the commented GRACE/GPM file-inspection examples, `xarray`; `rasterio` is also needed for ECOSTRESS raster reading). The Get Ready page lists `earthaccess requests xarray`; local Python had to install them before the search snippets could execute.[3][5]
- A free Earthdata Login, and appropriate authorization for the relevant DAAC, to download Earthdata Login products. Search success is not download access; the code leaves login/download calls commented out.[3][5]
- Actual study logic: choose a farm polygon and time period, retrieve/read bands, reproject/resample and mask data, handle missing/quality/cloud values, and translate observations into rotation risks. The snippets search/list data; they do not implement the “common grid” or compare rotations.[3]
- A browser developer console or an ES-module-capable Node context for the JavaScript snippet’s top-level `await`. It works in the browser console and the tested Node `-e` context; pasted into a classic `<script>` or a default CommonJS `.js` file, top-level `await` is not valid without module mode. This is an execution-context caveat, not a failure of its request logic.[3]
- POWER’s direct rainfall total is computed from returned values `>= 0`; using it in a crop comparison still requires choosing a season, handling missing-value conventions and explaining units/coverage.[3]

**Rotation-scoring prior art and status distinction.** Agreena is the clearest verified shipped rotation-index/score overlap: its current sign-up article names “bare soil and rotation indexes,” and its 2025 launch coverage says the Field Rotation Score measures five-year crop diversity. Those are vendor descriptions; the score formula and comparative agronomic validation were not published in the material read.[28][29]

OneSoil’s help material supports a rotation table and automatic allocation based on user-entered crop history; that is a shipped planning/recordkeeping feature, not evidence of an image-derived score.[20]

ADAS Crop Planner and RotationARM are live rotation-comparison tools, but the former is a UK whole-rotation economic/environmental model and the latter is APSIM simulation; neither was found to ingest satellite-derived field history.[30][31][32]

**Single clearest unoccupied gap (inference).** A no-login, farmer-correctable, field-bound rotation assessment that derives prior seasons from open Earth-observation evidence, compares the current rotation with alternatives against local soil/water constraints and farmer priorities, and shows per-season provenance, uncertainty and the reason each option scores as it does. This is a specific combination, not a claim that rotation software does not exist: the gap is the intersection of open entry and auditable observed field history with decision comparison across farmer priorities. The prior products each cover part of that combination.[20][28][29]

ADAS and RotationARM are comparison tools; I did not find them ingesting satellite-derived field history.[30][32]

## Winners teardown

**2024 — decision utility and use of data.** NASA’s 2024 announcement says NVS-knot assessed planting conditions with surface soil moisture and evapotranspiration and built an app to manage planting risk. GaamaRamma’s entry is described as helping farmers manage water availability amid drought, pests and disease.[11]

Treat the detailed flow as the VITech account, not as a current live product or independent evaluation.[Local report, §§1.2–1.3]

Screenshot evidence: `print-nasa-2024-winners.png` is NASA’s winner announcement, not a project UI; `print-gaamaramma-team.png` is a team/judging page; `print-vitech-2plant.png` is the VITech build article, not proof that the prototype remains online.[Local report, §§1.2–1.3]

The report states no public 2Plant repository or demo was located. The transferable pattern is one concrete decision per field, with limiting variables visible; it is not a prescription to copy a 2024 front end.[Local report, §§1.2–1.3]

**2025 — useful agriculture scope and product evidence.** On the Field Shift page, the Atlas matches Zumorroda-X’s “NASA Farm Navigators” and QUEÑARIS’s “BloomWatch” by theme; the Atlas itself labels those matches as its own judgment. The NASA announcement identifies Farm Navigators as an educational farming mini-game about adaptation to extremes and BloomWatch as a local water/ecosystem project; neither announcement says it solves crop-rotation planning.[3][6][12]

Screenshot evidence in the local report: `print-nasa-2025-winners.png` is an announcement, not an app; `print-spacegenes-dashboard.png` and `print-spacegenes-site2.png` show a dashboard and its product site; `print-resonant-site.png` shows a solution landing page; `print-nacle-app.png` shows a working research-catalog interface; `print-memoralab-site.png` shows a landing page; `print-terraview-repo.png` shows a repository/setup surface. These examples demonstrate what teams shipped in 2025—dashboards, deploys, sites and repos—not evidence that any one UI pattern guarantees an award.[Local report, §§3.2, 5.1–5.3]

**Reusable patterns, bounded by the evidence.** The local report’s strongest transfer is pairing a narrow user question with visible data provenance and an artifact a judge can actually inspect: a field decision and its evidence; a map/dashboard/repo; and a README/video fallback. Screenshots do not show model evaluation, confusion matrices, uncertainty bands or offline behavior, so the report’s “what is missing” audit is useful: do not infer validated performance from an attractive screenshot or an award announcement.[Local report, §§1.2, 3.2, 4.2, 5.1–5.3]

**What may not survive current judging (inference).** Copying only a polished dashboard, a chatbot, or a globe is a weak strategy: those are presentation patterns rather than proof of correct data use. The 2024–25 screenshots support the existence of a mix of interfaces and project artifacts, but do not themselves establish which elements the judges valued or whether a model was validated. For Field Shift, the distinguishing evidence should be an actual rotation comparison, a visible source/season trail, and honest uncertainty, not a generalized “AI agriculture” claim. (Inference from the evidence limits in the local report.)
