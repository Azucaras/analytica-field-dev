## S6.2 Input prices and the second-crop arithmetic

A national price signal can identify which crop deserves a closer budget, but it cannot calculate a farm's margin. Compare the whole sequence: sale price and basis, fertilizer already bought or applied, remaining nutrients, fuel and field passes, harvest and hauling, credit, and the planting window. Show each input's date and geography; let the grower supply local bids, yields and finance terms.

The AMS Illinois Production Cost Report PDF is dated September 28, 2026; the observations are for the week ending October 2, 2026. It quotes anhydrous ammonia at $945 per ton and farm diesel at $5.63 per gallon. It also listed DAP at $929.29 per ton, MAP at $936, urea at $770, potash at $516.36 and UAN28 at $466.67. These are dated Illinois observations, not a quote for every Corn Belt farm or for Brazil. [A November 2026 secondary report](https://dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/AG/crops/article/2026/11/04/uan28-continues-lead-fertilizer-week) put anhydrous at $1,007 per ton, but it is less firmly supported and should not be folded into the observed series as equally verified. Test whether nitrogen-intensive corn, especially corn after corn, covers its incremental nutrient and financing cost against soy or a legume cover. The grower must enter delivered bids, application rates, sunk preplant costs and expected yields before ranking those choices.

Expensive fuel need not force a crop switch. An October 2026 farmer account described reducing tillage under higher fuel prices, while cautioning that cutting fertilizer could cost more in lost yield. Compare fewer passes, reduced tillage and custom work alongside a crop change. A regional diesel quote cannot reveal a farm's pass count, machinery availability or weed pressure. Those local facts determine whether changing sequence is actually cheaper.

Revenue inputs also differ in status. On November 10, 2026, [DTN's report on USDA's November WASDE](https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/news/article/2026/11/10/usda-releases-november-crop-wasde) gave the 2026/27 season-average price estimates as $4.60 per bushel for US corn and $12.10 per bushel for soybeans (documented). Crop size, yield and stocks remained estimates, not final outcomes. The forecast ratio can flag soy for comparison with corn, but it is not a settled harvest price or a farm's elevator bid. [June 2026 NASS acreage and stocks](https://nass.usda.gov/Newsroom/2026/06-30-2026.php) are observations from an earlier vintage, not a reconciliation of the November estimates. Preserve each vintage and allow users to replace national figures with local bids and basis.

From July through October 2026, reported Brazilian rural-credit disbursement was R$107.6 billion, down 25.7 percent year on year, and included nearly R$20 billion in renegotiations. It was not all new working capital. This is a reason to use actual loan availability in a high-cost safrinha budget, not to assume a planned public envelope was drawn down. A lower-cash-cost crop or cover may be attractive when credit is scarce, subject to local buyers, costs and repayment dates.

Calendar can dominate a favorable spreadsheet. An Embrapa/UFMT experiment published in 2024 for Santa Carmem, Mato Grosso, found that corn sown on January 19 in 2018 yielded 2.33 megagrams per hectare more than corn sown on February 27. This is location-specific timing evidence, not a universal February cutoff or a 2026 yield prediction. Soy maturity and harvest date, equipment, ZARC and local weather determine whether a second crop fits. If late harvest pushes corn beyond the feasible window, compare sorghum, millet, sesame, sunflower, brachiaria, crotalaria or no grain second crop against their actual markets and costs. Commodity prices alone do not identify a winning rotation.
